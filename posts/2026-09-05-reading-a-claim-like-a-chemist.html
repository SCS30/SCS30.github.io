Reading a claim like a chemist

A patent claim and an experimental write-up look like the same kind of object. Both are precise, both are written by chemists, both describe a way of making something. They are not the same kind of object at all, and the gap between them took me a while to see.

When I read a paper, I read for what happened. The authors ran the reaction, and the write-up is a record of it: this catalyst, this solvent, this temperature, this yield. If the conditions are narrow, that is because the chemistry is narrow. Nobody specifies a temperature range of 20 to 200 °C in a paper, because nobody ran the reaction at every point in it.

A claim is not a record of what happened. It is a boundary drawn around what somebody else may not do. That changes almost everything about how the words behave.

The first thing it changes is what breadth means. In a paper, a broad scope is a claim about generality that the data has to support, and an over-broad one is a flaw a referee will find. In a patent, breadth is the point, and the wider the boundary the more it keeps out, but every widening is also a widening of the target. A claim that covers more art can be invalidated by more art. So a specification often reads as a set of nested fences: a wide independent claim, then narrower dependent ones behind it, each one a position to fall back to if the one in front is knocked down. Reading it as chemistry, this looks like indecision. Read as drafting, it is the opposite.

The second thing it changes is what a word costs. In a paper, comprising and consisting of are near-synonyms, and I would have swapped one for the other without much thought. In a claim they are close to opposites: the first is open, so a composition with extra components still falls inside; the second is closed, and the extra component puts you outside. About, substantially, essentially, words I would have edited out of a manuscript as vague, are doing real load-bearing work, and how much work they do has been argued over at length.

The third is the one I found hardest. A claim has to be read as though the person reading it were looking for the way out. The useful question is not does my compound fall within this? but what could someone make that reads on this claim, and what could they make that just misses? That is an adversarial reading, and it is not how I was trained to read anything. Chemists read generously. We fill in the steps an author left out, because we know what they meant. A claim gets no such charity, and should not be drafted expecting any.

None of this is unfamiliar territory once you have sat in the meetings. During my CASE placements at GSK and AstraZeneca, the questions that actually decided which chemistry we pursued were often not synthetic ones. A route could be elegant and still be the wrong route, because of what somebody else had already fenced off. At the time I found that faintly annoying. Now I think it was the most interesting thing in the room.

What I have found carries over well is the habit of reading a method until it can only mean one thing. A good experimental section and a good claim share that discipline, even if they point in opposite directions: one so the work can be repeated, the other so the boundary cannot be walked around. The chemistry got me interested. The precision is what made me want to change jobs.
